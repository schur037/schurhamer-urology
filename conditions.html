Prostate Cancer

Prostate cancer is one of the most common cancers in men — and one of the most treatable when found early. Many prostate cancers grow slowly, and not every one needs immediate treatment. The goal of modern care is to match the intensity of treatment to the actual risk of your cancer, while protecting urinary and sexual function.

How it’s diagnosed

  • PSA blood test — a screening tool; an elevated level prompts further evaluation, but does not by itself mean cancer.
  • MRI (mpMRI) and PSMA PET — advanced imaging that helps locate and characterize suspicious areas before any biopsy.
  • Targeted biopsy — sampling guided by imaging for greater accuracy.
  • Gleason score & genomics (Decipher) — tools that grade aggressiveness and refine your individual risk.

Treatment options

Depending on your risk, options range from active surveillance (close monitoring) to focal therapy (HIFU, irreversible electroporation, or cryoablation that treats only the cancer while sparing healthy tissue), to robotic surgery and radiation. I have a particular focus on focal and minimally invasive approaches that aim to preserve quality of life.

Bladder & Urothelial Cancer

Bladder and upper-tract urothelial cancer is a particular focus of my practice — including bladder-sparing treatment and the newest intravesical immunotherapies. Blood in the urine (even once, even painless) is the most common warning sign and should always be evaluated; most bladder cancers are found at an early, treatable stage.

How it’s diagnosed

  • Cystoscopy — a thin camera to look inside the bladder.
  • Urine testing and imaging of the urinary tract.

Treatment options

Care ranges from endoscopic removal of early tumors and intravesical (in-the-bladder) therapy — including modern gene- and immunotherapies such as nadofaragene firadenovec and cretostimogene — to surgery for more advanced disease. Ongoing surveillance is an important part of bladder cancer care.

Kidney Cancer

Kidney (renal) cancers are often found incidentally on imaging done for another reason. Small kidney masses can frequently be removed while preserving the rest of the kidney.

How it’s diagnosed

  • CT or MRI imaging to characterize the mass.
  • Biopsy in selected cases.

Treatment options

Options include active surveillance for small, low-risk masses, partial nephrectomy (removing the tumor while sparing healthy kidney), and minimally invasive or robotic surgery. Treatment is tailored to tumor size, location, and your kidney function.

Kidney Stones

Kidney stones are common and can cause sudden, severe pain. With fellowship training in endourology, I treat stones using minimally invasive techniques and focus on preventing them from coming back.

Treatment options

Depending on stone size and location: observation with pain control for small stones, shock-wave lithotripsy, ureteroscopy with laser, or percutaneous surgery for large stones — plus a personalized prevention plan.

Enlarged Prostate (BPH)

An enlarged prostate (benign prostatic hyperplasia) is a non-cancerous condition that becomes more common with age — affecting more than half of men in their 50s. It can cause bothersome urinary symptoms such as a weak stream, frequency, or getting up at night.

Treatment options

Options range from lifestyle and dietary changes and medications to a variety of minimally invasive procedures. Because BPH and prostate cancer can cause similar symptoms, it’s important to be evaluated rather than assume.

Male Urinary Incontinence

Urinary leakage can follow prostate surgery or develop with age. It’s common — and a wide range of effective treatments can restore control and confidence.

Treatment options

From pelvic-floor therapy and lifestyle measures to procedures and surgery (such as a sling or artificial sphincter) for the right candidates.
