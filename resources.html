Nutrition & Prostate Cancer

Ten things every patient should know. “Heart healthy is prostate healthy. Heart healthy is all healthy.” — Mark A. Moyad, MD, MPH

01 Heart Healthy = Prostate Healthy

Men with localized prostate cancer are more likely to die from cardiovascular disease than from the cancer itself. There is strong (Level 1) evidence that plant-based diets reduce cardiovascular disease — the same diets that benefit your prostate.

Takeaway: Ask your doctor about your cholesterol, blood pressure, and blood sugar at every visit.
02 Maintain a Healthy Weight

Obesity is linked to at least 13 cancers, including aggressive prostate cancer. Even slow weight loss of 1–2 pounds per week improves cancer-related biomarkers and survival.

Takeaway: A healthy weight matters more than which specific diet you follow.
03 Eat More Plants, Less Processed Food

Plant-rich diets are associated with a 19% lower risk of fatal prostate cancer and 47% lower risk of progression, while reducing processed and red meat (classified as carcinogenic by the WHO). Patients also report better urinary, sexual, and hormonal quality of life.

Takeaway: Aim for at least 5 servings of vegetables and fruits daily.
04 Limit or Eliminate Alcohol

Alcohol contains 7 calories per gram, promotes insulin resistance, and has been associated with more aggressive prostate cancer.

Takeaway: Many patients see meaningful weight loss simply by cutting out alcohol.
05 Exercise Is Medicine

Physical activity is one of the pillars of lifestyle medicine. Even brisk walking for 30 minutes most days reduces fatigue, helps manage hormonal-therapy side effects, and improves outcomes.

Takeaway: Find a safe routine you enjoy and can sustain.
06 Exercise Together When Possible

Group walks, classes, or exercising with a friend add mental-health benefits beyond exercising alone — especially for patients on hormonal therapy.

Takeaway: Join a walking group or find an exercise buddy.
07 Be Cautious with Supplements

No supplement is proven to cure or prevent prostate cancer. Selenium does not prevent it and may increase diabetes risk; vitamin D and omega-3s have not shown a clear cause-and-effect benefit.

Takeaway: Always tell your doctor about every supplement you take.
08 Calorie Management, Not Fad Diets

Whether Mediterranean, low-carb, or plant-based, the key is sustainable calorie management. Fad diets lead to rebound weight gain.

Takeaway: Choose an eating plan you can stick with for life.
09 Sleep, Stress & Mental Health Matter

Restorative sleep and stress management are pillars of lifestyle medicine. Depression and anxiety are common during treatment; nutrition, activity, sleep, and social connection build emotional resilience.

Takeaway: Caring for your mind and sleep is part of caring for your cancer.
10 Talk to Your Cancer Team

No dietary change replaces proven cancer treatment. Nutrition is a powerful complement to surgery, radiation, or hormonal therapy — not a substitute.

Takeaway: Bring your nutrition questions to your next appointment.

Adapted from patient-education work by Mark A. Moyad, MD, MPH (University of Michigan) and Stacy Loeb, MD, MSc, PhD (NYU Langone).

Questions About Supplements & “Natural” Approaches?

Patients often ask about saw palmetto, pumpkin seeds, and other remedies. The evidence varies a great deal — for example, no supplement is proven to prevent or treat prostate cancer, even where some dietary approaches may help benign prostate symptoms. Dr. Schurhamer is happy to review what the science does and doesn’t support for your situation.
